## Round 1: METHODOLOGY & SAMPLE

---

### 🦅 REVIEWER (Reviewer 2, the Hawk):

> **Major Concern #1: Inadequate Sample Size for the Analyses Conducted**
>
> The authors report N=152 for an instrument with 80 Guna items + 44 BFI items (124 total). This is a **subject-to-item ratio of 1.2:1**, far below the minimum recommended 5:1 ratio for factor analysis (Comrey & Lee, 1992) and the widely cited N≥300 threshold (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2007). Running an 8-factor EFA on 112 items with only 152 cases is statistically irresponsible. The factor solution is almost certainly unstable and would not replicate.
>
> How do the authors justify this?

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> We acknowledge that the sample size is a limitation and have stated this explicitly in the manuscript. However, several points merit consideration:
>
> 1. **The 5:1 rule is a heuristic, not a law.** Recent Monte Carlo studies (de Winter et al., 2009; Mundfrom et al., 2005) show that factor recovery depends more on **communalities** and **factor overdetermination** (items-per-factor) than raw N. With 26–27 items per Guna factor and communalities averaging 0.40+, factor recovery is feasible even at N=150.
>
> 2. **We do NOT claim to have confirmed a factor structure.** We explicitly label this as **exploratory** factor analysis. The purpose is to demonstrate that Guna-specific dimensions *emerge* from the data — not to confirm a pre-specified model. CFA on an independent sample is listed as a future direction.
>
> 3. **Our reliability and validity findings do not depend on EFA.** The Cronbach's α values (0.87–0.92), criterion correlations (r=.35–.46), and incremental validity (ΔR²=6.8–22.7%) are all computed on the full N=152 and are statistically robust with strong power.
>
> **However, we accept this criticism as valid.** We will:
> - Add explicit language noting the EFA results should be interpreted as **preliminary and requiring replication** with N≥400
> - Report KMO and Bartlett's test to demonstrate data adequacy
> - Acknowledge the subject-to-item ratio limitation prominently in the Discussion

✅ **Verdict: Accept criticism. Add caveats to EFA section. Core validity findings remain intact.**

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Major Concern #2: Convenience Sampling and Generalizability**
>
> The sample appears to be drawn primarily from a single educational context (66% male, 53% students, predominantly from similar age/education background). This is hardly representative of the Indian population. The authors claim the GPI captures "culturally specific dimensions" — but whose culture? Urban, educated, young males?

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> This is a fair point about generalizability, and we appreciate the reviewer raising it. However:
>
> 1. **This is an initial validation study**, not a norming study. The purpose is to establish psychometric properties and demonstrate proof-of-concept. Every personality instrument begins with convenience samples — the original BFI was validated on American college students.
>
> 2. **Despite the demographic homogeneity, we found striking variability.** Spiritual practice ranged from "Never" to "Regular" with large effect sizes (η²=.15–.22), and Gita familiarity spanned the full range. This suggests the instrument captures meaningful individual differences even within a relatively homogeneous sample.
>
> 3. **Gender neutrality is actually a strength**, not a weakness. The fact that no gender differences were found suggests the GPI is not biased by gender, which would be a serious concern if present.
>
> **We accept this limitation.** We will:
> - Explicitly state the demographic profile and its implications for generalizability
> - Recommend future validation across diverse regions (rural, different age groups, different SES)
> - Avoid overgeneralizing claims to "Indian culture" — instead frame as "preliminary evidence from an urban educated sample"

✅ **Verdict: Accept. Narrow the claims. Note as limitation. Core findings valid within sample.**

## Round 2: CONSTRUCT VALIDITY CONCERNS

---

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Major Concern #3: Circularity in Incremental Validity**
>
> The authors trumpet that Guna scores predict "Spiritual Practice" (ΔR²=+19.8%) and "Gita Familiarity" (ΔR²=+22.7%). But this is **tautological**! The GPI is built on concepts from the Bhagavad Gita, and its items reference spiritual behavior directly (e.g., "Spiritual advancement is very important for me," "I study books of traditional wisdom"). Of COURSE a spiritually-themed questionnaire predicts spiritual behavior — this is **method overlap**, not incremental validity.
>
> The scenario-based results are more convincing, but even there: 79.7% of respondents chose sattvic options. This is a **massive ceiling effect**. With almost no variance in the outcome, the correlations are difficult to interpret meaningfully.

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> This is the most challenging criticism, and we partially concede it. Let us address each part:
>
> **On the circularity concern:**
> We acknowledge that predicting spiritual practice from spiritually-themed items involves some content overlap. However, we push back on calling it "tautological":
>
> 1. **The Big Five also contains content overlap with its criteria** — Conscientiousness items ("I am systematic") predicting work performance is equally "circular" by this logic, yet this is accepted as valid criterion evidence.
>
> 2. **The GPI measures a personality TRAIT, not a behavior.** Items like "Spiritual advancement is important to me" measure an attitude/value, while "Spiritual Practice frequency" measures an actual behavior. Attitudes predicting behavior is the core of personality psychology — this is the **attitude-behavior link**, not circularity.
>
> 3. **If the relationship WERE merely tautological, Big Five would show a similar pattern.** But it doesn't — Big Five explains only 5.3–11% of spiritual practice/Gita familiarity variance. The *differential prediction* is the key finding.
>
> **On the scenario ceiling effect — we fully accept this criticism:**
> The 79.7% sattvic choice rate is indeed problematic. With such restricted range in the outcome, our correlations are likely **attenuated** (i.e., the true relationships are even stronger), but the ceiling effect limits interpretability.
>
> **We will:**
> - Reframe spiritual practice/Gita familiarity results as **supporting evidence** rather than primary evidence of incremental validity
> - Lead with the scenario-based results as the primary validity evidence
> - Acknowledge the ceiling effect in scenarios and note that future work should include scenarios with more balanced response distributions
> - Add more balanced scenarios (4–5 scenarios with counterbalanced desirability) in the next data collection

⚠️ **Verdict: Partially accept. The circularity criticism is overblown but has a grain of truth. The ceiling effect is a genuine problem. Reframe the argument accordingly.**

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Major Concern #4: Social Desirability Bias**
>
> Sattva scores are conspicuously high (M=5.05 on a 7-point scale, left-skewed). Tamas scores are conspicuously low (M=2.87, right-skewed). This screams **social desirability bias**. Respondents are clearly presenting themselves as more "sattvic" and less "tamasic" than they actually are.
>
> Without a social desirability measure (e.g., Marlowe-Crowne), how can you distinguish genuine Sattva from impression management?

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> This is a valid methodological concern. We respond on two levels:
>
> 1. **Social desirability affects ALL self-report personality measures**, not just the GPI. Big Five Agreeableness (M=3.96/5.0) shows a similar positive skew. This is an inherent limitation of self-report methodology, not specific to Guna measurement.
>
> 2. **The scenario-based criterion validity partially addresses this concern.** If the high Sattva scores were purely impression management, they should NOT predict behavioral choices in scenarios (which are less transparent in their scoring). The fact that Sattva scores DO predict sattvic scenario choices (r=.46) suggests genuine trait variance, not just social desirability.
>
> 3. **The Tamas scale still shows excellent reliability (α=.92) and meaningful variance** (SD=0.73), indicating respondents ARE willing to endorse tamasic items. A pure desirability response would show near-zero Tamas variance.
>
> **However, we accept we should have included a social desirability measure.**
> We will:
> - Acknowledge this as a limitation
> - Note that the scenario validation provides partial mitigation
> - Include a Marlowe-Crowne or BIDR scale in the next data collection
> - Consider adding subtle "validity items" to detect faking

⚠️ **Verdict: Accept the limitation. Defend with scenario evidence. Plan to add social desirability measure in future.**

## Round 3: STATISTICAL CONCERNS

---

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Major Concern #5: Multiple Comparisons Problem**
>
> The paper reports dozens of statistical tests across 8 phases (correlations, ANOVAs, post-hoc tests, regressions) without any correction for multiple comparisons. With this many tests, several "significant" results are likely false positives. Have you applied Bonferroni correction?

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> We respectfully push back on the blanket application of Bonferroni here:
>
> 1. **Bonferroni correction is appropriate for a family of tests addressing the SAME hypothesis**, not for an entire validation study. Our analyses address different research questions (reliability, validity, demographics) — applying Bonferroni across all of them would be overly conservative and statistically inappropriate (Perneger, 1998; Rothman, 1990).
>
> 2. **Our key findings survive even aggressive correction.** The core incremental validity results (ΔR² p < .001 for 4/5 outcomes) would remain significant under Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = .01). The criterion correlations (r=.35–.46, all p < .001) are far below any reasonable threshold.
>
> 3. **We emphasize effect sizes throughout**, not just p-values. Cohen's d values of 0.5–1.4 and η² values of 0.15–0.22 are large by any standard and cannot be dismissed as Type I error.
>
> **However, we will:**
> - Apply Bonferroni correction within each family of tests (e.g., within the 6 post-hoc comparisons in Phase 6)
> - Report both corrected and uncorrected p-values for demographic post-hoc tests
> - Lead with effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than relying solely on p-values

✅ **Verdict: Defend strongly. Core findings survive correction. Add correction for post-hoc tests only.**

---

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Concern #6: EFA Without CFA**
>
> You present EFA results but no Confirmatory Factor Analysis. In 2026, publishing a scale validation without CFA is below contemporary standards. How do you know the 3-factor Guna structure holds?

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> We agree that CFA is the gold standard for confirming factor structure. However:
>
> 1. **CFA requires an independent sample.** Running CFA on the same data used for EFA is equally poor practice (Worthington & Whittaker, 2006). Since we have a single sample of N=152, splitting it would leave both halves underpowered.
>
> 2. **Our EFA is conducted jointly with Big Five items**, which is a STRONGER test than a simple 3-factor CFA on Guna items alone. By including 44 BFI items as "competing" variables, we demonstrate that Guna factors emerge ABOVE AND BEYOND Big Five factors in the same analysis.
>
> 3. **The reliability evidence (α=.87–.92) independently supports the 3-scale structure.** You don't need CFA to demonstrate that items within each Guna scale are internally consistent and measure a common construct.
>
> **We fully accept CFA is needed.** It is the primary recommendation for future work. We are currently collecting additional data (target N=400) specifically for this purpose.

✅ **Verdict: Accept. CFA is a legitimate gap. Joint EFA is a reasonable intermediate step. Frame as planned next step with ongoing data collection.**

---

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Concern #7: BFI Agreeableness and Openness have poor reliability**
>
> BFI Agreeableness α = 0.582 is below the accepted threshold (0.70). Openness was 0.556 before refinement. How can you draw valid conclusions about Guna-BFI relationships when your BFI measure is unreliable?

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> This is a fair concern, and we must be transparent:
>
> 1. **Low BFI reliability is a known issue.** The BFI-44 uses short scales (8–10 items per trait).  Agreeableness α < .70 has been reported in multiple Indian samples (e.g., Srivastava, 2015), likely due to cultural differences in agreeable behavior construal.
>
> 2. **Low Agreeableness α actually ATTENUATES correlations with Guna traits.** This means the true Guna-Agreeableness relationship is likely STRONGER than what we report. Our finding of significant correlations between Tamas and Agreeableness (r=-.32) despite low reliability makes the result MORE impressive, not less.
>
> 3. **We addressed Openness through item refinement** (removing 3 problematic items, improving α from .556 to .731), demonstrating our commitment to measurement quality.
>
> **We will:**
> - Report reliability-corrected correlations (using Spearman-Brown disattenuation) as a supplementary analysis
> - Acknowledge BFI limitations and suggest using the NEO-PI-R or BFI-2 in future studies

✅ **Verdict: Defend strongly. Low BFI reliability actually strengthens Guna validity claims (attenuation argument). Accept for future improvement.**

## Round 4: THEORETICAL CONCERNS

---

### 🦅 REVIEWER:

> **Major Concern #8: What is the GPI actually measuring?**
>
> The "Material Desire" factor contains a SINGLE item ("I often feel greedy"). A one-item factor is uninterpretable. The "Spiritual Orientation" factor is dominated by religious behavior items. Are you measuring a personality trait or religious observance? Personality traits should be stable dispositions, not behavioral frequencies.

### 🧑‍🔬 RESEARCHER:

> This is a thoughtful critique. We respond:
>
> **On the single-item factor:** We concede this is a limitation of the current EFA solution. A single-item factor should NOT be interpreted as a stable dimension. We will reframe this: instead of claiming "2 unique Guna factors," we will report "1 robust unique Guna factor (Spiritual Orientation) and a preliminary second factor requiring additional items and replication."
>
> **On personality vs. religious behavior:** This is the deeper theoretical question. In Western personality psychology, traits are defined as stable cognitive-affective dispositions separate from behavior. But the Triguna framework does NOT make this separation — in Sāṅkhya philosophy, guṇas ARE the constitutive qualities of both mental dispositions and behavioral tendencies. They are ontologically different from Big Five traits.
>
> Rather than force the Guna framework into a Western personality mold, we argue the GPI measures **value-disposition complexes** — integrated patterns of values, attitudes, and behavioral tendencies that are conceptually coherent within Indian philosophical psychology.
>
> **However, we will:**
> - Remove the single-item factor claim from the abstract
> - Add a theoretical section discussing the trait vs. value-disposition distinction
> - Acknowledge that the boundary between personality trait and religious identity needs further theoretical work

⚠️ **Verdict: Partially accept. The single-item factor must be downgraded. The trait-vs-behavior question is a legitimate theoretical debate, not a flaw — but it needs to be addressed explicitly.**
